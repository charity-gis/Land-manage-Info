00

Why this document exists

The original PRD's build route recommended Python, Django and GeoDjango. The team has decided to build this instead on Node.js, React and Next.js — a single JavaScript/TypeScript language across the whole system, front to back. Almost none of what the PRD actually requires changes because of that decision: the database is the same, the legal workflow rules are the same, and the two architectural decisions that matter most (explained in §02) are the same. What changes is only which tools implement those requirements.

This document also assumes at least one person on the team has never worked with mapping/GIS software before. So every mapping term and every land-law term is defined in plain English the first time it matters, and there's a standalone glossary (§01) to come back to.

01

Plain-English glossary

Skip this section if you already know it. Come back to it whenever a term below shows up later in the doc — it will always appear in mono type the first time it's used in a section.

Mapping & data terms

Parcel
A single, legally defined piece of land with its own boundary and ID. It's the core "record" the entire system revolves around — everything else (ownership, disputes, listings) hangs off a parcel.
Geometry / boundary
The actual outline of a parcel's shape, stored as a connected ring of coordinates (a polygon) — not just one pin dropped on a map.
CRS (Coordinate Reference System)
The "ruler" that turns a real spot on Earth into numbers a computer can store. Different surveys use different rulers — using the wrong one silently shifts every parcel to the wrong place on the map, with no error message.
Shapefile
The near-universal file format government survey offices hand over (a .shp file plus a few hidden companion files). It's raw material to import from, not a finished database.
PostGIS
An add-on for our PostgreSQL database that teaches it to understand and search shapes, not just numbers and text — so we can ask "which parcels overlap this one?" directly in the database.
Vector tiles / tile server
Pre-cut "map puzzle pieces" that a small dedicated program slices out of the database on request, so a browser showing 500,000 parcels only ever downloads the pieces currently on screen.
GeoJSON
A JSON-based (i.e. JavaScript-native) way of describing a shape. Used to move parcel boundaries between the browser and the server.
Topology / geometry validity
Whether a shape is "legal" as a shape: no self-crossing outlines, no missing corners — and critically, no two parcels claiming the same ground, which is often literal evidence of double-selling land.
Cadastre / cadastral
The official government register — map plus ownership — of land. "Cadastral survey" is the formal land-measuring process behind it.

Legal & workflow terms

Chain of title
The ownership history of a parcel, transfer by transfer, back to its origin.
Encumbrance
Umbrella term for "something is legally blocking this parcel from being sold, leased or transferred." Severity increases left to right:
Charge / Mortgage→ Caution→ Restriction→ Inhibition
MoU (Memorandum of Understanding)
A signed, formal agreement with a government office. No technical integration work with their systems is allowed to start before this is signed.
Verification Officer / Senior Verifier
The platform's own trained staff who check ownership evidence before anything is published. A Senior Verifier is the required, independent second check on higher-risk cases.
Audit trail / hash chain
A permanent record of who changed what, and when — where each entry mathematically "locks in" the one before it, so tampering with old history becomes detectable.
02

What can't change, no matter the stack

Two decisions in the PRD are called out as things that "cannot be retrofitted" later. They have nothing to do with Python vs. JavaScript — they have to be right on day one regardless of language, so they come before any tooling discussion.

Principle A

Multi-tenancy from day one

Every parcel row carries a region_id (which city/county it belongs to) from the very first line of code — even though the pilot only ever uses one region. Adding this later means rewriting every table and every query in the system. Adding it now costs nothing, because there's only one region to worry about anyway.

Principle B

The verification gate is a database rule, not a promise

The database itself refuses to let a parcel's status become Verified from anywhere except the one verification workflow, and refuses to let it be listed for sale unless it already is. A bug, a rushed deadline, or a dishonest developer could all bypass a rule that only lives in application code. They can't bypass a rule the database enforces on every single save.

Pending Verification Verified Disputed Under Legal Hold

Every parcel starts in Pending Verification the moment it's imported. Nothing in the codebase — in any language — is allowed to move a parcel straight to Verified.

The stack, translated to JS

Row by row: what the PRD originally suggested, what replaces it in a Node.js/React/Next.js build, and why the swap is safe (or what to watch for).

Database PostgreSQL + PostGIS Unchanged The database doesn't know or care what language talks to it. No swap needed here at all.

Monorepo, in one sentence

One repository holding an apps/api folder (NestJS, 21 modules) and an apps/web folder (Next.js) that talk to each other over a normal API — not 21 separately-deployed services, not yet. See §06 for why that restraint matters.

04

The build route

Nine steps, each with a deliverable and an exit gate — the thing that has to be demonstrably true before moving to the next step. Steps 1–5 run in strict order and build almost nothing "product-shaped" on purpose: they exist to make sure the ground under the trust engine (Step 6) is solid. Steps 6–9 can partly run in parallel once that ground is proven.

01

Know your data

Before writing a line of the real app, we open the government shapefiles and figure out, honestly, what's actually inside them.

Checks performed
  • Feature count, and whether the CRS ("ruler") is correct — Kenyan cadastral data is commonly in UTM Arc 1960 zone 36S or 37S; the wrong one silently displaces every parcel.
  • Every column's name, type, how-often-empty, and value range.
  • Whether any column is actually a unique parcel ID.
  • Geometry validity: self-crossing outlines, unclosed rings, zero-area shapes.
  • Overlapping parcels — each overlap is a potential double-allocation, which is exactly the fraud class this platform exists to catch.
  • Whether anything resembling an owner's name exists at all, and how trustworthy it looks.
Built with
QGIS (free, no code)Node.js + shapefile (npm)
Exit gate

You can state, in one paragraph, exactly what these files contain and don't contain, compared to what the data model expects. Any bad surprise is far cheaper to find here than in Step 5.

02

The canonical schema

The actual database design — written down as real tables and rules before importing a single real parcel.

Key pieces
  • A region table, populated with exactly one row for the pilot city — but present from day one (Principle A, §02).
  • A parcel table: immutable ID, shape, region, land use, area, status, listing status, current owner, last-verified date.
  • A parcel_version table — every field change writes a new row; nothing is ever overwritten.
  • An audit_log table — append-only, hash-chained, written through one single function everything else must go through.
  • Empty "stub" tables for dispute and encumbrance, so the "can this parcel transact?" check (Step 7) has somewhere to look from day one.
  • A database-level rule that listing_status can only be set when status = 'Verified' — Principle B, made literal, not just checked in app code.
Built with
Prisma schema + migrationsOne raw SQL migration for the geometry column & CHECK constraints
Exit gate

A brand-new, empty database can be built from these files alone, and it is impossible — even hand-writing raw SQL — to list an unverified parcel.

03

The ingestion pipeline

The import script — built to run safely again and again, because it will: every new ward, every corrected survey, every new county later.

Rules
  • Convert every shape to one consistent storage ruler (EPSG:4326), but always compute area using the correct local projected ruler — never in raw degrees.
  • Broken shapes get repaired, and every repair gets logged.
  • Column-name mapping lives in one small config file — never hardcoded.
  • Rows that fail validation go to a quarantine table with a reason — never silently dropped.
  • Every loaded parcel remembers its source file, file hash, load time, and loader version.
Built with
Node.js + TypeScriptshapefileproj4@turf/turfpg
Exit gate

Load, truncate, reload — identical result, every time. Loaded rows plus rejected rows equals the source file's feature count, exactly.

04

Verify the load

A second, independent check — ideally written by someone other than whoever wrote the loader, so it can't share the loader's blind spots.

Checks
  • Feature counts and total area reconcile against the source, within tolerance.
  • A random sample of parcels actually appears in the right place on a real map.
  • Every parcel ID really is unique.
  • The audit-log hash chain validates end to end.
Built with
JestStandalone verification script
Exit gate

The report comes back clean, or every discrepancy has a written explanation next to it.

05

The map

The first thing anyone outside the dev team can actually look at: every parcel, real and searchable.

Honest detail

At this point every parcel is Pending Verification, so the map should be a wall of amber — and it should say so proudly, not apologize for it. That's the correct, honest state of the register this early. Owner-name search is deliberately missing: it's permission-gated, and there are no verified owners yet to search for.

Built with
Next.jsMapLibre GL JSreact-map-glMartin (vector tiles from PostGIS)
Exit gate

You can hand someone a link and they can find a specific parcel. This is the first artefact a government stakeholder can actually look at — which is why getting here fast, without cutting Steps 1–4, matters so much.

06

The verification engine

The actual trust machine the whole PRD is built around — the workflow no parcel can skip. This is why Steps 1–5 were kept deliberately thin.

The one non-negotiable design point

Once a Verification Officer approves a parcel, the system doesn't flip a switch directly. It broadcasts an event — "this parcel was Approved" — and the marketplace and ownership code are only ever listening for that event; they have no other function that can change a parcel's status. That's what makes the gate physically impossible to bypass by accident, not just against policy.

Also included
  • Document vault: encrypted storage, hashing, malware scan, per-file access logging.
  • Risk tiering: value, first-time submitter, prior disputes, registry mismatch.
  • Officer assignment — system-assigned, never self-selected.
  • Second-reviewer gate above the risk threshold, blocking publish.
  • Duplicate-document-hash detection across different claimed owners — cheap to build, catches a real and common fraud pattern.
Built with
NestJS modulesXStateS3-compatible storageRedis + BullMQ (event/queue)
Exit gate

A parcel can move from Pending to Verified only by traversing the full workflow, with a complete audit chain — and a deliberate attempt to find a shortcut around it fails, every time it's tried.

07

Encumbrances and disputes

Before the marketplace exists, build the one function every other part of the system must ask: "is this parcel actually free to be sold or transferred right now?"

Why now, before the marketplace

So there is never a moment in the marketplace's history where it doesn't consult this check. Not "we'll add the check later" — the check exists before there's anything yet to check against.

Built with
One shared NestJS service functioncharge / caution / restriction / inhibition tablesDispute intake with auto-freeze
Exit gate

Placing any one of these blocks immediately and provably stops every listing and transfer path for that parcel.

08

Marketplace and public portal

The part regular people actually see and use: listings, public search, the "Verify a Parcel" tool.

Every one of these is only reachable for parcels that are already Verified and unencumbered — because by this point, that's the only state the underlying code is even capable of producing.

Built with
Next.js routes/pagesMasked contact detailsOffer & counter-offer tracking
Exit gate

An independent penetration test, and a load test at ten times pilot-city volume, before this goes anywhere near the public.

09

The statutory workflows

The remaining legally-required processes: subdivision, ownership transfer, change of land use, succession (inheritance), easements.

Sequencing

Build in the order the pilot city will actually use them — most likely ownership transfer first, subdivision second, and succession third, since fraudulent succession claims are also one of the most common sources of real land fraud and need the most legal care.

05

The parallel legal track

Steps 1–5 are pure software and can start today. But the PRD assumes a collaborating government registry, and budgets 6–8 weeks for legal setup. That clock has to start now, in parallel with Step 1 — not at Step 6 — because it's the longest-lead item in the whole programme and gates nothing technical until Step 6. That's exactly why it's the item most likely to be forgotten until it's suddenly the critical path. None of this depends on which programming language the team chose.

  • Identify the pilot county's land registry and county physical planning office.
  • Open the MoU conversation — no technical integration work begins before a signed data-sharing agreement.
  • Establish where the shapefiles came from and under what licence they may be used — this determines whether anything can be published at all.
  • Data Protection Act 2019 compliance: register with the ODPC as a data controller, and produce a Data Protection Impact Assessment. Handling national ID numbers alongside land records is high-risk processing under the Act — a legal requirement, not a best practice.
  • Confirm data-residency/hosting-location constraints before picking a cloud region.
  • Agree — and repeat consistently to every stakeholder — the platform's position relative to the national system (e.g. Ardhisasa): an accelerator that reconciles against the official record, never a competing registry.
06

What not to build yet

Named explicitly, because each of these will be tempting.

✕

Blockchain audit anchoring

The hash chain (Step 2) already gives tamper-evidence. External anchoring adds outside attestation, which is worth nothing before there's anything worth attesting to.

✕

Payments and escrow

Explicitly out of scope for Phase 1, and it drags in financial-services regulation the team isn't set up to handle yet.

✕

The offline field app

A real future need, but it depends on the verification workflow (Step 6) existing first — building it earlier has nothing to plug into.

✕

21 separately-deployed services

Same trap in JavaScript as in Python: module boundaries now (§03), deployment boundaries only once real volume demands them.

✕

Owner-name search

Permission-gated, PII-heavy, and meaningless until there are actually verified owners to search for.
